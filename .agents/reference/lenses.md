# The lens catalog

The seven ways a phrase gets read during a review. The finder sweeps every
phrase through all seven; the verifier judges whether a flagged reading is one a
real member of the stated audience would actually take. This file is the
detailed reference — the skill body names the lenses and points here.

Each lens below gives: what it catches, the tell to look for, and an example of
a phrase → the misreading. The examples are illustrations, not a checklist to
match against literally.

---

## 1. Connotation

**Catches:** word choices whose emotional or social coloring fights the goal,
even when the denotation is right.

**Tell:** a near-synonym would shift how the writer or subject comes off. "Cheap"
vs "affordable", "aggressive" vs "ambitious", "childish" vs "playful",
"interrogate" vs "ask", "exploit" vs "use". Register mismatch counts here too:
slang in a formal notice, corporate jargon in a warm apology.

**Example:** "Our tool is a cheap alternative." → reads as low-quality, not
low-cost. Intended value (saves money) is undercut by the connotation of shoddy.

---

## 2. Lexical ambiguity

**Catches:** a word with more than one common sense where context doesn't force
the intended one.

**Tell:** the word has a second everyday meaning a skimmer might land on first.
"Sanction" (permit / punish), "oversight" (supervision / mistake), "dust"
(add / remove), "left" (remaining / departed), "table" (postpone / present).

**Example:** "The committee will sanction the new policy." → permit it, or
penalize it? Opposite readings, both plausible without more context.

---

## 3. Syntactic ambiguity

**Catches:** a sentence whose grammar permits a second parse — attachment,
scope, or coordination ambiguity.

**Tell:** a modifier, prepositional phrase, or negation could bind to more than
one thing. "I saw the manager with the report" (who has the report?). "All that
glitters is not gold" (scope of *not*). "We can offer refunds or credits and a
discount" (what does *and a discount* attach to?).

**Example:** "Tell the client the meeting moved on Monday." → did the meeting
move, on Monday I should tell them, or the meeting is now on Monday? Three
parses.

---

## 4. Double meanings

**Catches:** idioms, innuendo, unintended puns, homophones (for text read
aloud), and acronym or name collisions.

**Tell:** the phrase has a second, often lighter or cruder, reading that would
distract or embarrass. Unintended sexual/scatological innuendo in marketing,
a product name that reads badly, an idiom taken literally by a non-native
reader.

**Example:** "We're committed to going deep on your backend needs." → an
unintended innuendo that will get screenshotted.

---

## 5. Implicature

**Catches:** what the phrase implies beyond its literal content — the inference a
reader draws that the author never stated.

**Tell:** hedges, presupposition triggers, and agent-hiding constructions.
"I think this should probably work" → low confidence. "As I already mentioned" /
"per my last email" → impatience, you-weren't-listening. "Mistakes were made" /
"the file was deleted" → dodging responsibility (agentless passive). "Even you
can use it" → you're not the sharp one. "still" / "already" / "just" carry
presuppositions ("are you still working on that?" presupposes it's late).

**Example:** "As I said before, the deadline is Friday." → implies the reader
failed to absorb it the first time; reads as scolding in a peer email.

---

## 6. Audience-shifted readings

**Catches:** how the phrase lands for a reader who is not the author — a
non-native English speaker, a different culture, a hostile or skeptical reader,
or a fast skimmer.

**Tell:** the phrase relies on idiom, shared context, tone-of-voice, or
good-faith reading to land correctly. An idiom that inverts across cultures
("table this" — US postpone vs UK discuss now), a joke that reads flat in text,
a phrase whose charitable reading requires trusting the writer.

**Example:** "Let's table the budget discussion." → a US reader postpones it; a
UK reader brings it up now. Same words, opposite action.

---

## 7. Goal alignment (whole-text)

**Catches:** cumulative tone and emphasis that work against the stated purpose,
even when no single phrase is wrong.

**Tell:** run once over the whole text after the phrase sweep. Does the balance
of warmth vs firmness, detail vs brevity, confidence vs hedging serve the goal?
An apology that spends more words on justification than on the apology. A
persuasive pitch that leads with caveats. A decline so padded it reads as a
maybe.

**Example:** an apology email where four of five sentences explain why it
happened → reads as excuse-making, not contrition, regardless of the words.

---

## Severity

Every surviving finding carries a severity, and the bar to report is set by the
stakes of the text (see the skill body). Rough scale:

- **high** — a reader in the stated audience is likely to take the wrong meaning,
  and the cost is real (lost deal, offense, legal exposure, a dead link shipped).
- **medium** — a plausible misreading with moderate cost, or a likely misreading
  with low cost.
- **low** — a possible misreading a careful reader would resolve; report only
  when stakes are high or the fix is free.

A finding that is only reachable by a pedantic or bad-faith reader nobody in the
audience resembles is not a finding — the verifier exists to cut exactly these.
