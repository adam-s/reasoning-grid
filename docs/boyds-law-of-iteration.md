Short answer: it isn't, really — but there's a genuine point buried in the quibble, and it's worth separating the two.

The formal sense (mostly a quibble). Iteration and recursion are interchangeable at the level of computation — any loop can be written as a recurrence and vice versa. A loop that carries state forward is a recurrence relation: state(n+1) = f(state(n)). So calling the OODA loop "recursive" is technically defensible and technically uninteresting.

The substantive sense (where it bites). The Atwood/Sessions framing invites you to picture iteration as repetition — do the same thing again, just faster. But that reading makes the story incoherent. If each dogfight cycle were an independent repetition, a slightly stiffer flight stick would be a fixed small tax per cycle and nothing more. The MiG pilot loses because the tax compounds: fatigue from cycle n degrades his performance in cycle n+1, which costs more energy, which degrades cycle n+2. The output is the next input. That's not "repeat N times," it's a self-referential process with accumulation — and that's the part people are pointing at when they say recursion.

The mutual-recursion version. Boyd's actual claim was about getting inside your opponent's decision cycle. Your Observe step takes as input the results of his Act step, and vice versa. Two loops, each consuming the other's output — mutual recursion, not two parallel loops. The win condition isn't "finish more cycles," it's driving the opponent's state toward a base case (disorientation, then termination) faster than he can drive yours.

Where Sessions comes in. His article distinguished iterative from recursive approaches to complexity: iterating means refining the whole problem repeatedly; recursing means partitioning it into strictly smaller subproblems solved the same way, terminating at a base case. Atwood extracted only the slogan — "speed of iteration beats quality of iteration" — and dropped the decomposition half. So a fair criticism is that the blog post's takeaway ("when in doubt, iterate faster") flattens the original distinction rather than that Boyd's law was misnamed.

Worth noting the historical caveat too: Boyd's own account of F-86 vs MiG-15 (fast transients, energy-maneuverability) is contested by historians who credit pilot training, the gunsight, and the G-suit at least as much. The stiff-stick story is a good parable, not a settled finding.

It starts with Air Force&nbsp;<a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Boyd_(military_strategist)" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">Colonel John Boyd</a>&nbsp;researching a peculiar anomaly in the performance of 1950’s era jet fighters:</p><blockquote><p>Colonel John Boyd was interested not just in any dogfights, but specifically in dogfights between&nbsp;<a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikoyan-Gurevich_MiG-15" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">MiG-15s</a>&nbsp;and&nbsp;<a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-86_Sabre" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">F-86s</a>. As an ex-pilot and accomplished aircraft designer, Boyd knew both planes very well. He knew the MiG-15 was a better aircraft than the F-86. The MiG-15 could climb faster than the F-86. The MiG-15 could turn faster than the F-86. The MiG-15 had better distance visibility.</p><p>The F-86 had two points in its favor. First, it had better side visibility. While the MiG-15 pilot could see further in front, the F-86 pilot could see slightly more on the sides. Second, the F-86 had a hydraulic flight control. The MiG-15 had a manual flight control.</p><p>The standing assumption on the part of airline designers was that maneuverability was the key component of winning dogfights. Clearly, the MiG-15, with its faster turning and climbing ability, could outmaneuver the F-86.</p><p>There was just one problem with all this. Even though the MiG-15 was considered a superior aircraft by aircraft designers, the F-86 was favored by pilots. The reason it was favored was simple: in one-on-one dogfights with MiG-15s, the F-86 won nine times out of ten.</p></blockquote><p>How can an inferior aircraft consistently win over a superior aircraft? Boyd, who was himself one of the best dogfighters in history, had a theory:</p>

<blockquote>
<p>
Boyd decided that the primary determinant to winning dogfights was not observing, orienting, planning, or acting better. The primary determinant to winning dogfights was observing, orienting, planning, and acting&nbsp;faster. In other words, how quickly one could iterate.&nbsp;Speed of iteration, Boyd suggested,&nbsp;beats quality of iteration.</p>
<p>
The next question Boyd asked is this: why would the F-86 iterate faster? The reason, he concluded, was something that nobody had thought was particularly important. It was the fact that the F-86 had a hydraulic flight stick whereas the MiG-15 had a manual flight stick.</p>

Without hydraulics, it took slightly more physical energy to move the MiG-15 flight stick than it did the F-85 flight stick. Even though the MiG-15 would turn faster (or climb higher) once the stick was moved, the amount of energy it took to move the stick was greater for the MiG-15 pilot.</p>
<p>
With each iteration, the MiG-15 pilot grew a little more fatigued than the F-86 pilot. And as he gets more fatigued, it took just a little bit longer to complete his OOPA loop. The MiG-15 pilot didn’t lose because he got outfought. He lost because he got out-OOPAed.</p>
</blockquote>

<p>This leads to&nbsp;<strong>Boyd’s Law of Iteration – <em>speed</em> of iteration beats <em>quality</em> of iteration.</strong></p><p>You’ll find this same theme echoed throughout every discipline of modern software engineering:</p><ul><li>Unit tests should be&nbsp;small and fast, so you can run them with every build.</li><li>Usability tests work best if you&nbsp;make small changes every two weeks and <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20070217102609/http://www.uie.com/articles/fast_iterations/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">quickly discard what isn’t working</a>.</li><li>Most agile approaches recommend iterations&nbsp;<a href="http://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/article/view/30" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">no longer than 4 weeks</a>.</li><li>Software testing is about&nbsp;<a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20070212034622/http://blogs.msdn.com/micahel/archive/2005/08/17/FailFast.aspx" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank">failing early and often</a>.</li><li>Functional specifications are best when they’re&nbsp;<a href="https://blog.codinghorror.com/dysfunctional-specifications/">concise and evolving</a>.</li></ul><p>When in doubt,&nbsp;<em>iterate faster</em>.</p>
